## The name

> [Science is a bit like the joke about the drunk who is looking under a lamppost for a key that he lost on the other side of the street, because that's where the light is. It has no other choice.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Streetlight_effect#cite_note-11)

— Noam Chomsky, letter to Robert Barsky, 14 June 1993

Our philosophy is to start with experimentation and observation. The best research questions arise when we struggle to describe what we see in a clean mathematical formalism; it means we don't know the right math yet. We should choose to ask those questions anyway and _build the light_ where we need it.

## Expectations and Culture

We value transparency, openness, and active collaboration and communication.

### Preamble

Please read _[Some Modest Advice for Graduate Students](https://polypedal.berkeley.edu/?page_id=266#advice)_, especially if you're considering graduate school. It captures most of what I would tell you better than I could express it myself.

I also encourage you to watch _[Randy Pausch's Last Lecture](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ji5_MqicxSo)_.

### Expectations

Research is largely about deciding the right **destination**, choosing the **path**, and then doing the **work** of actually walking the path. These responsibilities roughly align with the expectations for **PhD candidates**, **master's students**, and **undergraduates**, respectively. As you progress, you inherit the previous expectations, but your priorities shift.

#### Undergraduates

For **undergraduates**, we will set the destination and path, and expect you to take ownership of getting the work done. We will help you along the way, offer advice, and help you learn the tools you need, but you are responsible for actually walking the path and getting to the destination. More concretely, you can expect to:

- Learn some skills
- Learn to be part of a team
  - Be reliable
  - Communicate actively
- Pick up good engineering habits
  - Take pride in your work.
  It’s not just about getting it to work; if another engineer looks under the hood, you want them to say, “A professional has been at work here.”
  - Fix the cause, not the symptom. Understand why!
  You won’t always be able to chase down every weird anomaly, but as an engineer, that should bug you, like an itch you can’t reach.

#### Master's students
{: #master-students }

For students pursuing a terminal master's degree, we will set the destination, and we will expect you to decide which path to take to get there. More concretely, expect to:

- Take ownership of solutions
  - Explain and defend your solutions
  - Take responsibility for your solution and see it through
  - Take responsibility for your learning path
  This is especially relevant here, but applies to every aspect of your path.
- Recognize good questions
  - Understand and explain why your project matters in the big picture
  Presenting your work to people outside the lab is an opportunity to practice this. Seek out these opportunities!
- Embody the lab values and contribute to lab functioning
  - Through engineering work, lectures, and day-to-day participation

#### PhD candidates

The mark of a **PhD** is that you are able to decide the destination: you need to take ownership of which questions to ask and why they're worth answering. This is a long journey, and we will work with you to get there.

More concretely, expect to:

- Contribute to the lab values and lab functioning
  - Help build the team culture through mentorship, day-to-day participation, and engineering work
  You're committing several years to this group. We want you to accept co-responsibility for the group and everybody's success.
- Take ownership of the question
  - Is it important to you?
  Do you want to devote multiple years of your life to this question, knowing that only a handful of people in the world may ever care about it? If too much of your validation is external, you'll run out of steam.
  - Is it important to the research community? Is it publishable?
  We're here to help you, but ultimately it is your responsibility to balance the values of the lab with the expectations of the university and the academic community.
  - Is it achievable?
  Importance and feasibility often pull in different directions. A PhD is a marathon: you need tenacity and perseverance for problems you believe are important, and the pragmatism and flexibility to abandon projects that show little promise. Grad school is very much about learning to make decisions in the face of uncertainty; we cannot take that uncertainty away from you.
- Manage your projects efficiently
  - Manage your time
  - Adapt and re-scope as you learn

---

I want to emphasize that *there is no stigma in deciding that you want to excel at one level without moving to the next.* In fact, society needs more people who excel at executing good solutions than people who excel at coming up with good questions to answer. For that matter, I think society needs more craftsmen and craftswomen than university graduates. One reason I decided to go to grad school is that, while I think I’m a good engineer, I enjoy coming up with interesting, weird questions more than answering them (although attempting to answer them is part and parcel of coming up with them).

If you're aiming for a PhD, we need to give you space to struggle and figure out what question you care about. We will start with a clear problem that lets you cut your teeth on owning a solution. As you develop as a researcher, you need to start reaching for ownership of the question; if you're working toward a master's along the way, that shift should usually begin before you've officially finished it. Getting the timing right is difficult and depends on the individual. Let's talk about it as we go.
